# Write an ICP a lead search can actually run: a one-page worksheet

*By Tarun Kumar · 16 November 2026*

"Small businesses that need marketing" is an ICP you can agree with in a meeting. It is not one you can search. A search needs nouns it can match: a type of business, a place, a visible signal, and a reason to leave someone out.

This ideal customer profile worksheet turns a fuzzy ICP into one or two sentences you can paste into any lead search, whether that is a map search, a database filter or a plain-language tool. Copy it, fill in the blanks, and keep the filled version next to your outreach notes.

## 1. Who: name the business type the way they name themselves

Write the category as it would appear on a shop sign or a map listing, not as a market segment.

- Weak: "healthcare SMBs"
- Better: "dental clinics", "physiotherapy centres", "diagnostic labs"

Fill in: **Business type(s):** `________________`

If you have more than three types, you probably have more than one ICP. Split them and run each one separately.

## 2. Where: pick an area you can actually serve

Name a city, a district or a few neighbourhoods. A smaller area that you can reach in person or by phone beats a whole country you can only email.

Fill in: **Area:** `________________`

## 3. What signal: the visible reason they need you now

This is the part most ICPs skip. A signal is something you can check from the outside, without asking the business:

- no website, or a website that is only a social media page
- a recent funding announcement
- hiring for a role your product replaces or supports
- a public developer profile or repository using a tool you integrate with
- few or no reviews on their map listing

Fill in: **Signal:** `________________`

Pick one signal per search. Stacking three signals in one query usually returns almost nothing, and you cannot tell which one filtered everyone out.

## 4. Exclusions: who looks right but is not

Write down the near-misses you already know about: chains and franchises if you sell to independents, businesses already using a competitor you cannot displace, or anyone outside the area you can serve.

Fill in: **Exclude:** `________________`

## 5. Put your ideal customer profile in one sentence

Combine the four answers:

> **[Business type]** in **[area]** that **[signal]**, excluding **[exclusions]**.

Examples:

- "Dental clinics in Gurgaon that don't have a website, excluding chains."
- "Funded SaaS startups in Bangalore hiring their first sales rep."

If you cannot read the sentence aloud without pausing to explain it, it is still too vague.

## 6. Judge the first results before you scale

Before exporting or messaging anyone, read the first page of results and mark each one:

- **Match**: fits every part of the sentence
- **Near miss**: fits the type and area, but not the signal
- **Wrong**: should never have appeared

Lots of near misses mean the signal is too loose; rewrite the signal. Lots of wrong results mean the business type is ambiguous; rewrite the business type. Only scale up once most of the first page is a match.

## 7. Keep a log

Note the sentence, the date and how the first page scored. Next month you will know which sentence produced customers and which only produced a long list.

## FAQ

**How is this different from a buyer persona?** A persona describes a person and their motives. This worksheet describes a business you can find from the outside, so a search tool can return it.

**Can one ICP have several signals?** Yes, but run one search per signal. You learn which signal produces matches, and an empty result is easy to diagnose.

**What if the first page is mostly wrong?** Fix the business type first, then the area, then the signal. Changing all three at once hides what helped.
